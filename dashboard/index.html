Executive summary

Two California properties, one inheritance expected in the 2040–2050 window, and three options: live in the inherited house, rent it, or sell it and reinvest. This dashboard assembles the verified rules, the verified rates, and the owner's own numbers, and then projects them forward. The headline conclusion is stable across every sensitivity tested.

Full reassessment to market value

The property tax reassessment is the whole story. Proposition 13 has kept Property A's assessed value at roughly $802,028 while its market value is projected to reach about $6,531,232. On a change in ownership the assessed value resets toward market. Annual property tax goes from about $7,200 today to about $69,763 if the heir moves in, or about $86,865 if the heir does not. That increase happens whether or not the exclusion is claimed — the exclusion only narrows the gap by about $17,103 a year.

Recommendation

Sell and reinvest produces the highest projected after-tax position 20 years after the transfer, at roughly $17,335,884, versus $7,168,260 for living in it and $10,052,372 for renting it. The margin is large enough to survive every sensitivity tested on the Scenarios page.

Why selling wins, in one paragraph

Property acquired from a decedent takes an income tax basis equal to its fair market value at the date of death (IRS Publication 551). That wipes out, permanently, all the gain that accrued during the decedent's lifetime. Selling soon after the transfer therefore realises almost no taxable gain, so the federal and California capital gains tax on decades of appreciation is never paid. Holding instead converts the asset into a position that pays roughly 1.33% of assessed value every year in property tax, plus insurance, maintenance and utilities, in exchange for rent or housing that does not cover it. Reinvested proceeds compound at an assumed 7% with tax deferred on the appreciation component. Over twenty years that difference is worth about $7,283,512.

Important details, in brief

Property tax: Prop 19 governs

Transfers on or after 2021-02-16 are governed by Proposition 19, not Proposition 58. The exclusion is available only for the transferor's principal residence, only if the heir makes it their own principal residence within one year, and only up to the factored base year value plus an indexed $1,000,000 (currently $1,044,586 for transfers through 2027-02-15). There is no exclusion at all for inherited rental property.

Property Tax Law →

Holding title in a trust does not help

The Board of Equalization defines a qualifying transfer as one "whether within or outside a trust." A revocable trust becoming irrevocable on the trustor's death is a change in ownership, and the date of death is the date of change in ownership. The residence, occupancy, value and filing tests all still apply.

Inheritance Process →

California has no death tax that collects anything

Revenue and Taxation Code section 13302 imposes a California estate tax only to the extent of the federal Credit for State Death Taxes. Congress repealed that credit, so the California tax computes to zero. Beneficiaries owe nothing to California for receiving the inheritance.

Inheritance & Estate Tax →

Federal threshold is $15,000,000 for 2026, permanent

OBBBA section 70106 set the basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 per person for calendar year 2026 and removed the sunset. It is indexed for inflation from 2027. The generation-skipping transfer exemption matches it. A surviving spouse can use a deceased spouse's unused exclusion, but only if a timely Form 706 is filed.

Inheritance & Estate Tax →

Two deadlines decide thousands a year

File the Homeowners' Exemption (BOE-266) within one year of the transfer, and file BOE-19-P within three years, or before transfer to a third party if earlier. Miss either and the exclusion is lost. Separately, Santa Clara County requires a Death Statement within 150 days.

Inheritance Process →

Moving out later removes the exclusion

Prop 19 requires continual use as a family home. If the heir moves in, claims the exclusion, and later rents the house out, the exclusion is removed and the new base year value is enrolled. The "move in for a year, then rent it" strategy does not work.
